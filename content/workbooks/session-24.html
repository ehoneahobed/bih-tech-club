1. Plain Talk: The Stage Curtain vs. The Actor

🎬 TRANSITION (The Stage Curtain)

A transition is the visual effect that happens when you move from one whole slide to the next slide.

Analogy: Opening or closing the physical theater curtain between Scene 1 and Scene 2.

Examples: Fade, Push, Wipe.

🎭 ANIMATION (The Actor on Stage)

An animation is movement applied to a single item inside a slide (such as one line of bullet text or a photo).

Analogy: An actor walking onto the stage while the curtain is already wide open!

Examples: Appear, Fade, Fly In.

Figure 24.0: Transitions move between whole slides; Animations reveal individual points inside a single slide.

2. The "No-Carnival" Rule for Animations

🎪 AVOID CIRCUS CLOWN ANIMATIONS!
Beginners often make words spin in circles, bounce like rubber balls, or zoom in with screeching tire sound effects. This distracts the audience and makes your school presentation look childish.

The Professional Rule: Stick to simple, gentle effects like "Fade" or "Appear". Use animation only when you want to hide points so the audience doesn't read ahead of you!

2. The Secret "B" Key: All Eyes on You!

💡 THE PROFESSIONAL SPEAKER'S SECRET WEAPON:
Sometimes during your talk, you want to tell a personal story or make an important community plea, but the audience is distracted reading your slide.

Tap the letter B on your keyboard once!
The entire projector screen turns pitch black. The audience has nothing to look at except YOU! When you are ready to show your slides again, tap B a second time and your slide returns instantly.

2. Delivery Secrets: Pacing & Voice

Pillar The Trap to Avoid What a BIH Tech Leader Does
1. Pace Rushing through words like an express tro-tro because you are nervous. Breathe. Pause for 2 seconds after each major bullet point to let ideas sink in.
2. Volume Whispering so only the front desk can hear you. Speak from your chest so the student at the very back of the classroom hears every word.
3. Eye Contact Staring at your shoes or reading word-for-word from your laptop. Sweep your eyes slowly: look at the left row, then center row, then right row.

1. Ethical Presenting: Citing Your Sources

In tech and academia, using someone else's statistics or photos without giving them credit is wrong. Always add a small, subtle Source Citation at the bottom of your slide:

Figure 24.3: A small footer tells the audience where each fact came from. These numbers are made up for practice, so the footer says so honestly.

2. How to Handle Questions from the Audience

When you finish your talk, teachers and classmates will raise their hands to ask questions. Follow the 3-Step Question Method:

1
Listen Carefully: Do not interrupt the questioner. Wait until they finish speaking completely.
2
Say "Thank You": Start with: "Thank you, Mr. Mensah, that is a great question." This gives your brain 3 seconds to think!
3
Answer Simply (or Admit You Will Research):
If you know the answer, state it clearly in 1 or 2 sentences.
If you don't know: Never make up lies! Say: "That is an excellent point. Our team has not tested that yet, but we will research it for our next session."
