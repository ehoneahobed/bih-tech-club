Session 27: Design with Canva

Every week you walk past posters, church and mosque notices, birthday flyers and WhatsApp statuses. Some you read at once; some you ignore. Today you will learn why, and then design your own poster, flyer and social media graphic for a real club or community event using Canva, a free online design tool.

1. Plain Talk: Design Is Like Arranging a Market Stall

Picture two tomato sellers at Makola Market. Auntie Esi piles everything in one heap: tomatoes, onions, pepper, empty sacks, her phone. Auntie Naa puts her best tomatoes in front, groups the onions in neat rows, leaves space so you can see each pile, and hangs one clear sign: "FRESH TOMATOES – GH₵10 a bowl". Who gets more customers?

A design is a stall for information. Good design puts the most important thing in front (the focal point), lines things up neatly (alignment), leaves breathing room (white space) and has one clear sign everybody can read from far away (the title).

Figure 27.0: The same information, two designs. Your eyes know where to go on the right.
🎨 WHAT IS CANVA?
Canva is a free design website (canva.com) with thousands of templates (ready-made designs you can change), pictures, shapes and fonts. Today you sign in with the Hub's learning account your mentor gives you, never your own or a parent's account. Some schools use Canva for Education, which is free for schools and has extra features.
The Design Basics
Session 27 • Page 2

1. Before You Touch the Computer: Purpose and Audience

Designers always answer three questions first. Write them on paper before you open Canva:

🎯 1. PURPOSE

What is it for? To invite, to inform, to sell, to warn?

👥 2. AUDIENCE

Who will read it? Pupils, parents, market traders, grandparents? Old eyes need bigger letters.

👉 3. ACTION

What should the reader do after reading? Come on Saturday, call a number, bring a bottle?

2. Five Rules That Make Any Design Look Good

RuleWhat it means
Focal pointOne thing grabs the eye first: a big title or one strong picture. If everything shouts, nothing is heard.
HierarchyThree levels of importance: the title (biggest), the key information (what, when, where; medium) and the details (smallest).
AlignmentLine things up along an invisible edge. Canva shows pink guide lines when an item is lined up with another.
White spaceEmpty space is not wasted space. It lets each part breathe, like the gaps between piles on a good stall.
Less is moreOnly what the reader needs. Leave out the extra sentences; put them on a website or say them in person.

Fonts, Colour & Templates
Session 27 • Page 3

1. Fonts: Two Is Enough

A font is a style of letters. Use no more than two fonts in one design: one bold font for the title and one plain, easy-to-read font for everything else. Too many fonts look like a heap of different stalls.

✅ DO

• A strong heading font (for example Montserrat or Anton) plus a simple body font (for example Open Sans).
• Make the title much bigger than the rest.
• Keep body text big enough to read from a metre away when printed.

❌ DON'T

• Curly "handwriting" fonts for long sentences.
• ALL CAPITALS for whole paragraphs.
• Stretching text boxes so letters look squashed.

2. Colour and Contrast

In Session 23 you learned that slides need high contrast: dark text on a light background, or light text on a dark background. Posters follow the same law. Pick 2–3 colours and use them again and again (this is called a colour palette).

Figure 27.2: Contrast. If you have to squint, change the colours.

3. Templates Without Looking Like Everyone Else

A template is a ready-made design you change to fit your event. Templates save time, but if 20 pupils pick the same one, 20 posters look the same. Give your template a makeover:

Done?Template makeover checklist
Replace every piece of sample text (no "Lorem ipsum" or "Your Company" left behind!).
Change the colours to your own palette of 2–3 colours.
Swap the sample picture for one you are allowed to use that fits your event (see Page 4).
Delete things you don't need, then move or resize parts so the layout is yours.
Check the fonts: two at most.
👑 THE LITTLE CROWN
In Canva, items with a small crown are Pro (paid) items. On a free account they may print with a watermark or ask you to pay. Choose free items instead, or use the filter in the side panel to show free items only.
Pictures You Are Allowed to Use
Session 27 • Page 4

1. Copyright: Somebody Owns That Picture

When a person takes a photo, draws a picture or writes a song, the law says it belongs to them. This is called copyright. Finding a picture on Google does not mean it is free to use, just as finding a phone on a trotro seat does not make it yours.

Some creators kindly share their work with a Creative Commons (CC) licence. A licence is permission with rules. The most common rule is "BY": you may use it if you credit the creator (write their name). Other rules can say "not for selling" (NC) or "don't change it" (ND).

Figure 27.3: The picture traffic light. When in doubt, leave it out (or ask your mentor).

2. How to Give Credit

Put a small line at the bottom of your design (Level 3 size): Photo: Name of creator, licence, where you found it. Example: "Photo: Kwame Boateng, CC BY, via Wikimedia Commons." Canva's free library items don't need a credit line, but you still must not pretend you made them yourself.

🚫 NEVER USE SOMEONE'S PHOTO WITHOUT PERMISSION
A photo of your friend, your teacher or a stranger is not just a picture, it is a person. Always ask first, and never put a photo of anyone (including yourself) in a school uniform on a design that will be shared online. This is called the School Uniform Rule, and you will learn why in Session 33.

2. Lab Mission: Design for a Real Event

Your team chooses one event: the Tech Club Open Day, a community clean-up, a reading club or a football match. Each pupil makes all three designs. Your mentor will tell you which ones get printed.

1
Plan on paper: write the purpose, audience and action, and the title, key information and details (fictional phone numbers only).
2
Sign in: go to canva.com and sign in with the Hub's learning account. Click Create a design (or + Create; it may look slightly different) and search Poster. For an exact A4 page, choose Custom size and type 21 × 29.7 cm.
3
Poster: in the left side panel open Templates, pick one and give it the full makeover (Page 3 checklist). Add pictures from Elements or Photos, or your own from Uploads.
4
Name and download: click the design name at the top and rename it, e.g. TechClub_OpenDayPoster_AmaMensah. Then Share → Download, choose PDF Print, and download.
5
Flyer: create a new Flyer design (A5 is half of A4, good for handing out). A flyer can carry a few more details than a poster. Download as PDF Print.
6
Social media graphic: create an Instagram Post (Square), 1080 × 1080 px. Copy your title and picture across (Ctrl+C, Ctrl+V). Keep it short: it will be seen on a small phone screen. Download as PNG.
7
Print: open the poster PDF and print one copy (or your mentor prints the team's best). Check it from two metres away: can you read the title?
