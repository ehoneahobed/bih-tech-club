Session 11: The Internet & Your Browser

Until now, your computer has worked alone inside our classroom. When you connect it to the Internet, it joins a worldwide network linking millions of schools, libraries, hospitals and homes on every continent. Today you will learn how that network works and how to drive the program that opens it up for you: the web browser.

1. Plain Talk: The Road Network of Computers

Think about the road network in Ghana: small feeder roads connect villages to highways, and highways lead to big cities like Accra, Kumasi and Tamale. Trotros, taxis and lorries travel along these roads carrying people and goods.

The Internet is an electronic road network: millions of computers connected by cables, radio masts, Wi-Fi and giant undersea glass (fibre) cables laid under the Atlantic Ocean. Several of these cables come ashore on Ghana's coast near Accra and carry our messages to the rest of the world in a fraction of a second.

Figure 11.0: Your Hub PC sends a request through the router, along the network and undersea cables, to the server that stores the web page.

2. Internet vs. World Wide Web: Don't Confuse Them!

The Internet (The Roads) The World Wide Web (The Vehicles on the Roads)
The huge worldwide network of computers, cables, routers and radio signals. The billions of web pages, pictures and videos that travel across that network.
Analogy: the roads, bridges and junctions. Analogy: the trotros and lorries carrying passengers and goods.

The Web is not the only thing on the Internet roads: email (Session 13), WhatsApp messages and video calls travel on them too.

The Web Browser (Your Window on the Web)
Session 11 • Page 2

1. What is a Web Browser?

To visit a website, you need a program called a web browser. It fetches web pages from servers and turns their code into the words, pictures and buttons you see. All browsers do the same job; they just look a little different.

💻 ON A COMPUTER

Google Chrome (colourful circle), Microsoft Edge (blue-green wave, comes with Windows 11), Mozilla Firefox (orange fox) and Safari (a compass, on Apple Macs).

📱 ON A PHONE

Chrome on most Android phones, Safari on iPhones, plus others such as Samsung Internet, Opera and Firefox. The buttons may sit at the bottom of the screen.

Figure 11.1: The parts of a browser window (Chrome-style). Edge and Firefox put the same buttons in nearly the same places.

Web Addresses: URLs Decoded
Session 11 • Page 3

1. What is a URL?

Every home in Ghana can have a GhanaPost GPS digital address (like GA-183-9024). In the same way, every page on the Web has its own unique address called a URL (Uniform Resource Locator). The main part of the address, the website's name, is called its domain.

Figure 11.2: Anatomy of a web address (URL). example.org is a made-up teaching address. The extension gives a clue about who runs the site.

2. Domain Extensions: Who Runs the Website?

The last few letters of a domain give a clue about what kind of organisation made it:

3. The Security Padlock: HTTPS vs. HTTP

🔒 THE MEANING OF THE PADLOCK
Look at the left end of the address bar. A small padlock (newer Chrome shows a small "settings" icon in the same place) and an address starting https:// mean the connection is secure: what passes between your computer and the website is scrambled into secret code, so snoopers on the network cannot read it. If you see "Not secure", never type a password, PIN or personal details there. But remember: the padlock protects the journey, not the truth. A scam website can have a padlock too (more in Session 12).

3. On a Shared Computer: Leave No Trace

🕶️ PRIVATE WINDOWS

Chrome Incognito and Edge InPrivate: Ctrl+Shift+N. Firefox Private Window: Ctrl+Shift+P. When you close it, the browser forgets that window's history and sign-ins. It does not make you invisible: the websites and the network can still see what you visit, and downloads stay on the PC.

🚪 BEFORE YOU LEAVE

1. Sign out of every account (click your picture or name, then Sign out). 2. Never tick "Remember me" or let the browser save a password on a shared PC. 3. Clear your traces: Ctrl+Shift+Delete opens Clear browsing data (history, cookies). 4. Close the browser.

Guided Hands-On Lab: The Browser Skills Circuit
Session 11 • Page 5

1. Lab Mission: Complete the Circuit

Work through all eight skills in order. Tick each one and write the time on the clock when you finish it. Your team earns a point for every skill done correctly, and a bonus point if every member of the team finishes. Warm up first in the Internet Town 3D lab on your session page: its missions practise tabs, the bookmark star and downloads.

1
Go direct: Open Chrome or Edge. Click inside the address bar, type en.wikipedia.org and press Enter. You reached the site without using a search engine.
2
Tab juggling: Press Ctrl+T twice. In one new tab type ges.gov.gh; in the other type ug.edu.gh. Switch between all three with Ctrl+Tab. Close one with Ctrl+W, then bring it back with Ctrl+Shift+T.
3
Bookmark it: In the Wikipedia tab, search Wikipedia for Kakum National Park. Press Ctrl+D, change the name to Kakum - Club, choose the bookmarks bar (Edge: Favourites bar) and press Done.
4
Find and zoom: Press Ctrl+F and type canopy. How many times does it appear? Zoom to 150% with Ctrl++, then go back to 100% with Ctrl+0.
5
Download: Right-click the main picture of the park and choose Save image as… (Edge: Save image as). Name it Kakum_Photo_YourName. Press Ctrl+J to see it, then open File Explorer (Win+E) > Downloads and find it there too.
6
History hunt: Close the Wikipedia tab. Press Ctrl+H and reopen the Kakum page from your history.
7
Go private: Open a private window (Ctrl+Shift+N; Firefox: Ctrl+Shift+P). Notice the dark look. Is your bookmark still on the bar? Close the private window.
8
Leave no trace: Press Ctrl+Shift+Delete and read the options. Only press Clear data if your mentor says so; otherwise close it. Show your mentor your bookmark and downloaded picture.
