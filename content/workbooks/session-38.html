Session 38: Build a Web Page

Since Session 11 you have visited hundreds of web pages. Today you switch sides: you become a web builder. You will plan a small website about your community, build it with Google Sites, publish it for the Hub, and then peek behind the curtain at HTML, the language every web page is written in.

1. Plain Talk: A Website Is a Compound House

Think of a family compound house. At the front is the gate with a signboard: that is the home page, the first page visitors see. Inside are rooms: those are the other pages. A corridor joins all the rooms: that is the menu (navigation bar). The doors that take you from room to room, or out to another house, are links. And just as every house can have a GhanaPost GPS digital address, every website has a web address (URL) so people can find it.

Figure 38.0: The parts of a website. A site is a set of pages joined by a menu and links, found at one web address.
📄 Web page

One single page, like one room. For example, the "Food" page.

🏠 Website

A group of pages that belong together, with one name and one address, like the whole compound house.

1. Three Planning Questions

No mason builds a house without a plan. Before you open Google Sites, answer three questions:

🎯 1. Purpose

Why does this site exist? "To tell visitors about the good things in our town."

👥 2. Audience

Who will read it? "Pupils at the Hub and visitors to our town." Write for them.

🗂️ 3. Pages

What rooms does the house need? Keep it small: 3 or 4 pages is plenty.

A sitemap is a quick drawing of all the pages and how they connect. It is like the family tree you drew for folders in Session 4: the home page is at the top.

🛡️ THE WEB BUILDER'S SAFETY RULES
1. No personal details: no full names, phone numbers, house addresses, your school timetable or your route home (Session 33's P.I.I. shield).
2. No faces without consent: use photos of places and things, not people, unless you have their (and a parent's) permission.

4. Publish to the Hub only, not the whole world, unless your mentor says otherwise.
Building with Google Sites
Session 38 • Page 3

1. The Google Sites Editor

Google Sites is a free website builder. You don't write any code: you click, type and drag, like making a slide in Session 23. Go to sites.google.com and sign in with the Hub's learning account.

Figure 38.2: The Google Sites editor: your page on the left, the Insert, Pages and Themes tabs on the right, and Preview (👁) and Publish at the top. It may look slightly different.

2. The Six Builder Moves

MoveHow to do it
1. Start a siteClick Blank site (+). Type the site name top-left (for example Our Bozoma) and click the big title on the page to change it.
2. Add contentInsert tab → Text box, Images (Upload) or Map (search for a place, then Select). Drag blocks to move them.
3. Add a linkSelect some words, press Ctrl + K (or the 🔗 button), paste or choose the address and click Apply.
4. Add pagesPages tab → + at the bottom → New page → type its name → Done. It appears in the menu by itself.
5. Choose a themeThemes tab → pick one theme and one colour for the whole site. Every page then matches.
6. Preview and publishClick Preview 👁 and use the phone, tablet and computer buttons to check every size. Then Publish (see Page 5).
💡 GOOD WEB DESIGN = GOOD SLIDE DESIGN
Use the same rules you learnt for slides in Session 23: short text, big clear headings, strong contrast, one theme and colour scheme, and pictures that help the words. Most people will read your site on a phone, so always check the phone preview.
Behind the Curtain: A First Look at HTML
Session 38 • Page 4

1. What Is HTML?

Google Sites writes the code for you, but every web page in the world is really made of HTML (HyperText Markup Language). HTML uses tags in angle brackets to tell the browser what each piece of the page is: a heading, a paragraph, a picture or a link.

Figure 38.3: An HTML element. Most tags come in pairs: an opening tag and a closing tag with a slash.

2. Four Tags to Start With

TagWhat it makes
<h1>…</h1>The main heading. (<h2> is a smaller heading, like the Heading styles in Session 17.)
<p>…</p>A paragraph of text.
<a href="…">…</a>A link. href holds the address it goes to; the words between the tags are what you click.
<img src="…" alt="…">A picture. src is the picture file; alt describes it for people who can't see it. No closing tag.
Figure 38.4: The browser reads the tags (left) and draws the page (right). The tags themselves stay hidden.
🔗 HTML AND YOUR SCRATCH CODE
HTML describes a page; it has no loops or IF blocks like your Scratch games in Sessions 36–37. But the same law applies: the computer does exactly what you write. A missing / or > is a bug. Debug it like a coder: spot it, trace it, fix one thing.
