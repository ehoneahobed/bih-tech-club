Session 12: Search Like a Pro

The Web has billions of pages. Somewhere out there is the answer to almost any question you can ask, and also a lot of rubbish, adverts and rumours. Today you will learn to find the right page fast, and to tell a trustworthy source from a fake one. That is a skill you will use for every project in school and beyond.

1. Plain Talk: The Market Queen Who Knows Every Stall

Imagine you need a blue padlock in Makola Market. You could walk past thousands of stalls for hours. Or you could ask the market queen, who knows exactly which stalls sell padlocks, and which sellers are honest.

A search engine is the market queen of the Web. Long before you ask, its robot programs visit billions of pages and write down what is on each one in a giant catalogue called an index. When you type your keywords, it looks them up in the index and lists the best matches first.

Figure 12.0: How a search engine works. The first result is the best match for your words, not always the truest page.

2. Browser vs. Search Engine: Don't Confuse Them!

🪟 THE WEB BROWSER (Your Window on the Web)

The program on your computer or phone (Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari). It is the window you look through to visit any web page. You met it in Session 11.

👑 THE SEARCH ENGINE (The Market Queen)

A website you open inside the browser: Google, Bing or DuckDuckGo. Typing words (not an address) in the address bar also sends them to a search engine: Chrome usually uses Google, Edge uses Bing.

2. Search Operators: Your Power Tools

Operators are special symbols or words that give the search engine exact orders. They work in Google, Bing and DuckDuckGo.

3. Reverse Image Search: Is This Photo Old or Fake?

A reverse image search searches with a picture instead of words. It shows where else that picture appears on the Web, so you can find out whether a "today" photo is really years old, or from another country.

🔍 HOW TO DO IT

Chrome: right-click the picture and choose Search with Google Lens (the wording may differ). Any browser: go to images.google.com, click the camera icon, then upload the picture or paste its link. Bing Images has a camera icon too.

🕵🏾 WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Pages showing the same picture (look for "Exact matches"). Which is the oldest? What did the first page say the picture shows? If it was online years ago, it is not "today's" news. Edited and AI-made fakes (deepfakes) come in Session 40.

Can You Trust It? Sources & Fact-Checking

1. Reliable Sources vs. Rumour Sites

✅ Good places to start (Ghana)🚩 Warning signs of a rumour site
Government ministries and agencies (.gov.gh): e.g. the Ghana Education Service ges.gov.gh, Ghana Statistical Service statsghana.gov.gh, Ghana Health Service ghs.gov.gh.No author's name and no date.
Universities (.edu.gh): e.g. ug.edu.gh, knust.edu.gh, ucc.edu.gh.A shocking headline in CAPITALS, "SHARE BEFORE THEY DELETE THIS!!!"
Established national news: e.g. the Ghana News Agency, the Daily Graphic, GBC. They name their reporters and correct mistakes.A strange address such as ghana-breaking-news24.example.net, lots of pop-ups and adverts.
Encyclopaedias like Wikipedia: a good start, but check the references at the bottom.No other trustworthy site reports the same story.

3. Citing a Source: Show Where You Found It

To cite a source means to write down where a fact came from, so your teacher (or anyone) can check it. Write three things: the title of the page, the website, and the date it was published or updated (or "no date"), plus the day you visited.

📝 EXAMPLE CITATION
"Kakum National Park" · Wikipedia (en.wikipedia.org) · last edited (the date at the bottom of the page) · visited 14 November 2026

🚨 POP-UP TRAP: "YOU HAVE WON A FREE PHONE & 5,000 CEDIS!"
While searching you may see flashing boxes saying you won a prize, or that your computer has a virus. Rule: if you did not buy a ticket, you did not win! Never click inside the box and never type your name, school or phone number. Close the tab (Ctrl+W) and tell your mentor.
