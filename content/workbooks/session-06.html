Session 6: Inside the Computer: Processor, Memory & Storage

In Session 1 you met the outside of a computer: the monitor, keyboard, mouse and system unit. Today we open the box (in our imagination and in the 3D lab, never with a screwdriver!) and meet the three parts that do the real work. By the end you will know why some computers are fast, why some are slow, and why a power cut can eat your work.

1. Plain Talk: The Chop Bar Kitchen

Picture the kitchen behind a busy chop bar. Orders keep coming in: "One fufu and light soup! Two waakye!" Three things make the kitchen work:

Figure 6.0: The chop bar kitchen. The cook works only on what is on the worktable; ingredients are fetched from the storeroom.

💡 THE SMALL-TABLE PROBLEM
If the worktable is tiny, the cook keeps putting things back and walking to the storeroom for more. The food comes out slowly, even with a fast cook! A computer with too little RAM does the same: it keeps swapping things to the storage drive, and everything feels slow.
Memory vs Storage: The Worktable & the Storeroom
Session 6 • Page 2

1. Two Kinds of "Space" in a Computer

People often mix these two up, because both are measured in GB. They are very different jobs.

🍲 MEMORY (RAM) = the worktable

• Very fast, but small (usually 4 to 16 GB).

• Holds only what you are working on now: open programmes, open files, open browser tabs.

• Temporary: it is wiped clean when the computer shuts down or the power goes off.

📦 STORAGE = the storeroom

• Much bigger (often 256 GB to 1 TB or more), but slower to reach.

• Keeps everything: Windows, your programmes, saved documents, photos, music.

• Permanent: files stay there when the power is off, until you delete them.

2. What Happens When You Open, Work and Save

Figure 6.1: Opening copies a file from storage onto the worktable (RAM). Only saving copies your changes back to the storeroom.
⚡ WHY DUMSOR EATS UNSAVED WORK
When the lights go off, a desktop PC switches off at once and the worktable (RAM) is wiped. Every sentence you typed since your last save was only on the worktable, so it is gone. Whatever you saved is safe in the storeroom. This is why Session 4 taught you the habit: press Ctrl + S every 5 minutes!

How Big Is It? The Size Ladder
Session 6 • Page 3

1. Bits and Bytes

Deep down, a computer stores everything as tiny on/off switches. Each one is a bit (it is either 0 or 1). Eight bits make one byte, which is enough to store one letter, such as A. From there, each step up the ladder is about 1,000 times bigger (exactly 1,024 inside the computer, but 1,000 is close enough for us).

Figure 6.2: The size ladder, from a single bit to a terabyte.

🛟 THE TWO-PLACES RULE (BACKUPS)
Any important file should live in at least two places, for example on the PC and on a pen drive or in the cloud. A copy kept in case the first one is lost is called a backup. Drives break, phones get stolen, pen drives fall in the gutter. A backup means you shrug instead of cry.

2. File Types and Extensions

The letters after the dot in a file name are its extension. They tell Windows which programme opens the file.

.docx Word document
.xlsx Excel spreadsheet

.pptx PowerPoint slides
.pdf PDF (looks the same everywhere)

.jpg photo or picture
.mp4 video
.zip packed folder

Show extensions in File Explorer: Windows 11: View → Show → File name extensions. Windows 10: View tab → tick File name extensions. When you rename a file, never change or delete the extension, or the file may not open.

3. ZIP Files: A Packed Bag

Figure 6.3: A ZIP file packs many files into one smaller file. "Extract All" unpacks it into a normal folder.

The club's study packs are ZIP files. To unpack one: right-click the .zip file → Extract All… → Extract. A new normal folder appears next to it. Always extract first, then open the files from the new folder.

2. Lab Mission: Find Your Computer's Parts

Every computer can tell you what is inside it. You will be a detective and write a spec sheet (a list of the specifications, or main facts, about a machine).

1
Open About: Click Start → Settings (the gear) → System → scroll down and click About. (Shortcut: Windows + Pause on some keyboards.) It may look slightly different on Windows 10.
2
Read the Device specifications: find the Device name, Processor and Installed RAM. Copy them into the table below. Under Windows specifications, find the Edition (for example Windows 11 Home).
3
Check the storage: press Windows + E to open File Explorer, then click This PC. Under the Local Disk (C:) bar you will see something like "120 GB free of 237 GB". A bar that turns red means the drive is nearly full.
4
Do the sum: a class photo is about 4 MB. How many class photos fit in the free space? (Free GB × 1,000 ÷ 4.)
