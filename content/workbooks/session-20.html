Session 20: Functions, Ranges & Visual Charts

In Session 19, you multiplied single cells together using =B2*C2. But what if your school store sells 100 different items? Typing =D2+D3+D4+D5...+D101 would take 20 minutes! Today, you will learn how to command the spreadsheet to calculate entire columns in a single breath using Functions and Ranges.

1. What is a "Range"? (The Power of the Colon :)

A Range is a block of connected cells. In spreadsheet language, we write a range using the first cell address, a colon (:), and the last cell address.

The colon simply means "THROUGH TO":

Figure 20.0: Anatomy of the SUM function. The colon (:) tells the computer to add every cell from D2 to D5.

2. Formula vs. Function: What is the Difference?

FORMULA (Manual Math)

A math equation you write by hand using basic symbols: =B2*C2 or =A1+A2+A3.

Best for simple calculations between 2 or 3 cells.

FUNCTION (Preset Magic Word)

A built-in preset shortcut word provided by the spreadsheet to crunch massive lists of numbers: =SUM(D2:D5).

Best for adding or averaging 5, 50, or 5,000 cells at once!

The "Core 4" Statistical Functions
Session 20 • Page 2

1. Meet the Big Four Built-in Functions

In school, business, and science, these four functions do most everyday data work:

Function Name What It Does How to Write It in a Cell
=SUM(...) Adds every single number in the range together to calculate a Grand Total. =SUM(D2:D5)
=AVERAGE(...) Adds the numbers and divides by the count to find the mean (typical) score. =AVERAGE(C2:C5)
=MAX(...) Scans the entire list and displays the highest (maximum) value. =MAX(B2:B5)
=MIN(...) Scans the entire list and displays the lowest (minimum) value. =MIN(C2:C5)

Turning Numbers into Pictures (Charts)
Session 20 • Page 3

1. Why Do We Use Charts?

Human beings are visual thinkers. When a chief, headteacher, or market board inspects a spreadsheet with 500 rows of raw numbers, their eyes get tired. But when you convert that data into a Chart, patterns, trends, and comparisons leap out in one second!

2. The Three Classic School Chart Types

📊 COLUMN / BAR CHART

Uses vertical or horizontal bars to compare separate categories side by side.

Best for: Comparing sales of different items, attendance across classrooms, or harvest yields per village.

🥧 PIE CHART (Slices of a Cake)

A circle sliced into wedges showing parts of a 100% whole.

Best for: School budget spending (food, books, sports) or waste composition (plastics vs. organic food scraps).

📈 LINE CHART

Joins points with a line to show change over time. Best for: Rainfall or school attendance month by month.

Figure 20.2: Anatomy of a Column Chart. Data Labels on top of each bar display exact quantities.
The Golden Rule of Chart Selection
Session 20 • Page 4

1. What Cells Do I Highlight Before Making a Chart?

A chart is only as smart as the cells you feed into it. The computer does not guess: you must highlight the exact labels and numbers you want to visualize.

🚨 THE #1 BEGINNER CHART MISTAKE:
NEVER INCLUDE THE "GRAND TOTAL" ROW IN YOUR CHART!
If you include the Total row, the Total bar will be huge (e.g. 50 items) and will dwarf all your individual products, making them look like tiny flat slivers!
Figure 20.3: Data selection rule for charts. Always select the headers and items, but stop before the Total row!

2. Troubleshooting Chart Issues

Problem Observed Why It Happened Immediate Fix
My chart is completely blank! You highlighted empty white cells before clicking Insert Chart. Delete chart (Del), highlight your actual data table, and re-insert.
The bars have no labels under them. You only selected Column B (numbers) and forgot to select Column A (item names). Select both columns: from A1 across to B5!

Using Google Sheets or LibreOffice Calc? The buttons look a little different but do the same job.
